Advertising review: Larkmoor Aesthetics

Checked on 14 August 2026: website (25 pages), Google Business Profile, Instagram bio, highlights and the last 30 posts.
Against: the UK's published guidance on advertising prescription-only medicines (POMs).

Dear Larkmoor team,

Thank you for sending your details. Your consultation process is sound: the booking page asks for a consultation before any prescription treatment and names your prescriber. The problem is the advertising around it. "Botox" is on your homepage, in your Google results, on Instagram and in a time-limited offer, so a patient can find the product name without looking for it.

I've listed 11 things below, in the order I'd tackle them. If you only have an hour this week, do 1, 2 and 4. The offers and the homepage are what a complaint would be built on. Items 5 to 8 can go in the same pass, and 9 to 11 can wait for your next site update.

Each item quotes what's there now and gives wording you can paste in. It's your decision what to change. This isn't legal advice and doesn't say you've broken the law. If anything's unclear, reply to this email and I'll explain.

1The homepage names Botox, with a price and an offer

Website · homepage banner and the "Popular treatments" tiles

The first line on your homepage is "Book your Botox and lip filler appointment today", and the first tile below it prices Botox from £160 with "smooth away lines in 15 minutes". The guidance says a named prescription-only medicine shouldn't appear on a homepage at all.2, 3, 7 The price and the speed claim make the tile read as promotion. You can keep the 20% new-client offer, as long as it covers non-prescription treatments only.

Now
"Book your Botox and lip filler appointment today. New clients save 20%."
"Botox from £160: smooth away lines in 15 minutes."
Suggested
"Book a consultation for lip fillers, skin treatments, or lines and wrinkles. New clients save 20% on skin and filler treatments."
Tile: "Lines and wrinkles: talk to our nurse prescriber about the options that suit your face. Consultation £30."

2Google shows a Botox price and offer before anyone opens your site

Google Business Profile · a service, a post from 2 August, and the search snippet for /prices/

Your listing reaches people who never visit the website, so it's held to the same rules. Right now a search for "aesthetics clinic larkmoor" shows a Botox price in the snippet, a Botox service with a price, and an August offer post. Offers on the price of a medicine are the clearest problem in this review.1, 5, 7

Now
Service: "Botox: 1 area £160"
Post: "Baby Botox August offer, just £130 this month only"
Snippet: "Treatment prices in Larkmoor. Botox from £160, lip filler from £250…"
Suggested
Service: "Consultation for lines and wrinkles: £30, redeemable against treatment"
Post: delete it. If you replace it, promote the consultation, not a product or a price.
Snippet: "Treatment prices in Larkmoor. Lip filler from £250, skin peels from £85, consultations from £30. Nurse prescriber led."

3A treatment page called "Botox Larkmoor"

Website · /botox-larkmoor/, linked from the main menu, 19 mentions of "Botox"

This page is the closest match to an ASA ruling I've seen on a clinic site: a page titled "Best Botox in London" that kept naming the product was found to promote it to the public.4 Prices next to "anti-wrinkle injections" count as an ad for the medicine too.5 I'd turn it into a consultation page and move the prices to the plain price list (item 11).

Now
Title: "Botox Larkmoor | Best Anti-Wrinkle Injections"
"Why choose us for your Botox? We're Larkmoor's most trusted injectors."
Suggested
Title: "Lines and Wrinkles Consultation Larkmoor | Nurse Prescriber"
"If a prescription treatment is suitable, your prescriber will explain it, including risks and alternatives."
New address /lines-and-wrinkles/, with a 301 redirect from the old one so links and search results still work.

4A pinned Instagram offer on Botox

Instagram · carousel post from 9 August, pinned to the top of the grid

Posts and captions are ads under the ASA's rules, and this one is an offer on a medicine.1, 7 Even without the word, a syringe emoji next to a price can point to the product.5 Unpin and archive it, and take the product-name hashtags off this and older posts.

Now
"August offer 💉 3 areas of Botox for £199. Limited slots, DM to book!"
Hashtags: #botox #babybotox #larkmoor #aesthetics
Suggested
"Thinking about lines and wrinkles? Book a consultation with our nurse prescriber to talk through your options. Link in bio."
Hashtags: #larkmoor #aestheticsclinic #skinconsultation

5Instagram bio names Botox

Instagram · profile bio

The bio is the first thing on your profile, so it's held to the same rule as a homepage.1, 3 It's a one-word change.

Now
"Botox • Fillers • Skin | Larkmoor"
Suggested
"Fillers • Skin • Consultations | Nurse prescriber led | Larkmoor"

6A website testimonial names Botox

Website · /reviews/

The ASA names testimonials as a place a POM shouldn't appear.3 Don't edit a patient's words. Take this quote off the website and pick one that doesn't name a product. The original can stay on Google.

Now
"Best Botox I've ever had, so natural!"
Suggested
Remove from the website. Replace with another review.

7Lip flip page implies a toxin

Website · /lip-flip/

"Lip flip" can only mean a toxin treatment, and the ASA says "relaxing" is read as a reference to Botox.5, 6 I'd fold this page into the lip filler page and say other options are discussed at consultation.

Now
"A tiny dose relaxes the muscle so your lip rolls out"
Suggested
"Other options for the shape of your lips are discussed at consultation."

8Image alt text names Botox

Website · 6 before-and-after images

Alt text and file names show up in Google Images and screen readers, and the ASA lists hover text as a place POMs shouldn't appear.3

Now
alt="Botox before and after forehead"
Suggested
alt="Forehead, before and after treatment". Rename files such as botox-ba-1.jpg too.

9Filler FAQ compares filler to Botox

Website · /dermal-fillers/, FAQ

Lower risk, because it sits on a filler page and answers a real question. It still names the product for no reason.

Now
"Is filler the same as Botox?"
Suggested
"Is filler the same as other injectable treatments? No. Your prescriber will explain the difference at consultation."

10A Google Q&A reply gives a Botox price

Google Business Profile · questions and answers

You can't stop people asking, but your reply is your advertising. Keep the answer to the consultation.

Now
Q: "Do you do Botox?" Reply: "Yes, from £160!"
Suggested
"Yes, we offer consultations for lines and wrinkles with our nurse prescriber. Prices are given at consultation."

11Price list linked from the main menu

Website · /prices/

A plain price list is allowed, as long as it's informational and not easy to stumble on.2 A menu link on every page makes it easy. Link to it from the consultation page instead, and keep the wording plain.

Now
Main menu → "Anti-wrinkle, 1 area £160"
Suggested
"Prescription treatment for lines, 1 area £160", with no claims, linked from the consultation page.

What's already fine

  • The booking page asks for a consultation before any prescription treatment, and names the prescriber.
  • The "Is it safe?" page says botulinum toxin is a prescription-only medicine. That's safety information, not promotion.
  • Skin, filler and peel pages don't mention any POM.

What I didn't check

  • Paid ads (Google Ads, Meta ads), printed leaflets and signs in the clinic.
  • Instagram stories older than 24 hours, and posts older than the last 30.
  • Claims about treatments that aren't POMs, such as filler results.

Sources

  1. CAP Code, rule 12.12"Prescription-only medicines or prescription-only medical treatments may not be advertised to the public."
  2. CAP Bitesize: Rules for advertising Botox"A consumer casually browsing your site should not be able to find any reference to Botox easily."
  3. ASA Advice Online: botulinum toxin products"No reference to a POM should be made in a sponsored ad, on the homepage of a website, in logos, testimonials or hover text."
  4. ASA ruling: Glowery Ltd, 2023A page "titled 'Best Botox in London'" that "repeatedly referenced Botox" "promoted Botox to the general public".
  5. ASA Enforcement Notice, 2020"Anti-wrinkle injections" next to a price for a POM "will be seen as an ad for that POM".
  6. ASA FAQs, 2020"The word 'relaxing' is likely to be understood as an implied reference to Botox."
  7. MHRA Blue Guide, Appendix 6The home page "should not include any reference to named POMs". "Special offers on prices of medicines should not be highlighted."
