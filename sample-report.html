Prescription-only medicine references in Larkmoor Aesthetics' advertising

About this sampleLarkmoor Aesthetics, its website, Google listing and Instagram account are invented, and every quote below was written for this sample. The patterns are the ones I find most often on real UK clinic sites. A paid review has the same layout, with live links and real screenshots.

ScopeThis review compares the clinic's advertising with the UK's published guidance on advertising prescription-only medicines (POMs). It is not legal advice, and it does not say the clinic has broken the law. The new wording is a suggestion, and no ranking outcome is promised.

01

Summary

11 items to change across three channels

The consultation process is sound. The problem is the advertising around it: "Botox" appears on the homepage, in Google results, on Instagram and in a time-limited offer. A patient can find the product name without looking for it.

4High: change first 4Medium: same pass 3Review: lower risk

If you only do three things this week

  1. End the Instagram and Google offers on toxin treatments. Offers are the most direct conflict with the guidance. H2, H4
  2. Take "Botox" off the homepage and out of the Google search snippet. These are what a casual browser sees first. H1, H2
  3. Rename the /botox-larkmoor/ page and redirect the old address. H3

06

The rules used

Seven published sources, cited as R1 to R7

RefSourceWhat it says
R1CAP Code rule 12.12"Prescription-only medicines or prescription-only medical treatments may not be advertised to the public."
R2CAP Bitesize: Rules for advertising BotoxBotox may appear on a website as "a potential outcome of the consultation" or "in your price list", "purely informational" and "not on your homepage". "A consumer casually browsing your site should not be able to find any reference to Botox easily."
R3ASA Advice Online: botulinum toxin products"No reference to a POM should be made in a sponsored ad, on the homepage of a website, in logos, testimonials or hover text."
R4ASA ruling: Glowery Ltd, 2023A page "titled 'Best Botox in London'" that "repeatedly referenced Botox" "promoted Botox to the general public".
R5ASA Enforcement Notice, 2020"Anti-wrinkle injections" next to a price for a POM "will be seen as an ad for that POM".
R6ASA FAQs, 2020"The word 'relaxing' is likely to be understood as an implied reference to Botox."
R7MHRA Blue Guide, Appendix 6The home page "should not include any reference to named POMs". "Special offers on prices of medicines should not be highlighted."
07

What's fine, and what's not covered

Already fine: keep these

  • The booking page asks for a consultation before any prescription treatment, and names the prescriber.
  • The "Is it safe?" page says botulinum toxin is a prescription-only medicine. That is safety information, not promotion.
  • Skin, filler and peel pages make no POM references.

Outside this review

  • Paid ads (Google Ads, Meta ads), printed leaflets and in-clinic signs.
  • Instagram stories older than 24 hours, and posts older than the last 60.
  • Claims about non-POM treatments, such as filler results.
